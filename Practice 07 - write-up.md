**My Visual and My Write-Up for Practice 07**

**Takeaway**

Air quality in a coastal Asian city is not one number; it is a rhythm with two tempos, and the difference between them is what matters. The visual makes the fast tempo visible first: every day contains two traffic peaks, one at 08:00 when the city moves to work and school and one at 19:00-20:00 when it moves home, with the cleanest air in the small hours and, in the warm season, in the early afternoon. It then makes the slow tempo visible: a four-month winter monsoon season (November-February) runs at roughly three times the wet-season level (period means of 23.2 vs 7.1 µg/m³), so 94% of winter hours sit above the WHO 24-hour guideline of 15 µg/m³, against 6% between May and September. The takeaway is that breathing here is governed by the calendar far more than by the clock. Choosing to run at 06:00 rather than 08:00 buys a modest improvement (about 44% lower on weekdays); choosing to run in June rather than January buys a change several times larger. Three consequences follow for anyone reading or acting on this chart. First, personal advice of the "avoid rush hour" kind is a rounding error next to seasonal exposure. Second, short extreme episodes - the Lunar New Year fireworks, a December cold front - push single hours past 60 µg/m³ and would vanish inside any daily or annual average. Third, the morning peak is traffic, not weather: it nearly disappears at weekends. The design below exists to let a reader see all three of these at one glance, judged against the same threshold.

**Why a calendar heat map**

The dataset is assumed: hourly PM2.5 for one year (8,760 values) at a single urban monitoring site in a Macau-like coastal city. The assignment permits a simulated dataset, and I chose one deliberately, because the design question - how do you show a full year of hourly air quality without hiding its structure? - is better answered with dense, gap-free data than with a record whose missing hours would drive the layout. Those 8,760 values could be drawn as a line chart, but at one point per hour a line chart collapses into a solid block of ink and obscures exactly the structure the reader needs. I therefore used a calendar heat map: one row per day (365 rows, chronological, January at the top), one column per hour of the day (24 columns), and colour as the only quantitative channel. The whole year then fits into a single rectangle that the eye can scan in two directions at once. That orientation is itself a design decision: with days vertical and hours horizontal, the daily rhythm appears as vertical stripes and the seasonal cycle as horizontal bands, so the reader can decompose the two tempos with no arithmetic and no second chart. Chronological ordering is essential to the story, since any sorting that grouped similar days together would destroy the seasonal tide; the calendar order is the transformation.

**Colour and threshold**

The colour scale runs from a near-white neutral through pale teal-green, sand and amber to orange, red and deep maroon. Luminance rises monotonically with concentration, so the intuition "darker is worse" is correct at every point on the ramp, and the change of hue is a second, redundant cue that makes the crossing of each band easy to locate. I avoided a rainbow scale, in which equal steps in value do not look like equal steps. A bare colour ramp still leaves the reader asking "is that a lot?", so three anchors tie colour to meaning. The scale spans 0 to 61 µg/m³, the year's highest hour, so the top of the ramp corresponds to an actual event rather than an arbitrary ceiling. It is mildly power-compressed (exponent 0.85), which keeps the clean season's small but real differences distinguishable without distorting their order. And the WHO 24-hour guideline (15 µg/m³) is drawn as a labelled contour that cuts the colour bar and continues into the companion panel, so every cell can be read against a public health threshold rather than against its neighbours.

**Companion panel**

The heat map's density is also its weakness: each column holds 365 values, but the reader sees a distribution and not its centre or its spread. Below the map I therefore plotted the same year averaged by hour of the day - two median lines, weekdays and weekends, with the weekday inter-quartile range shaded behind them. Three decisions matter here. First, the lines are identified by a small legend of line swatches rather than by colour alone, so the pairing survives greyscale printing. Second, splitting weekday from weekend is the cheapest possible test of the causal story: a meteorological morning peak would persist at weekends, and this one does not - the 08:00 median falls from 20.9 to 11.6 µg/m³, about 44% lower. Third, the inter-quartile band is drawn but kept deliberately pale, so the reader notices the two lines first and the uncertainty second; the band exists to stop a median being over-read as a typical morning.

**Annotation, and what this design obscures**

A single bold title poses the question and the subtitle answers it with the two headline numbers (94% versus 6%), so the chart can be understood before the write-up is read. Episode annotations sit on the data rather than in a distant legend: the Lunar New Year band and the December cold front carry their own labels, in white or black according to the background they sit on. Month names double as the y-axis, which removes a redundant scale, and faint rules at 06:00, 12:00 and 18:00 mark the daily cycle while the month rules mark the seasonal one, so every filled pixel belongs to a temporal coordinate. What the design hides is equally deliberate. The city is treated as one point, so no roadside-versus-hillside difference is visible. Each cell is an hourly mean, so within-hour spikes are smoothed away. The ramp is capped at its top value, which compresses the very worst hours relative to the months around them. And a single year supports no claim about change over time. I added no rain, wind or holiday layer, because each would need its own encoding and would compete with the main channel; the design pays for its clarity with a story it can show but cannot prove.
