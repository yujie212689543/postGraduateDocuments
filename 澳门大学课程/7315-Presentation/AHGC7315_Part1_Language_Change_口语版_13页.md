## Slide 1 — The line has not changed. English has.

**Target:** 0:00–1:10  
**Slide cue:** Juliet comic → modern misunderstanding → `wherefore = why` → three ideas

OK, I'd like to start with one line from Shakespeare. Juliet says, “Wherefore art thou Romeo?”

Many of us would think she is asking, “Romeo, where are you?” It sounds reasonable. But imagine Romeo standing right below the balcony. He is not lost.

So, the word *wherefore* does not mean “where.” It means “why.” Juliet is asking why Romeo has to be Romeo, and why he has to carry that family name.

The line has not changed. English has.

Today I want to show you three things. First, sound changes follow patterns. Second, the order of those changes matters. And third, when English lost many word endings, grammar found new ways to do the same work.

*Delivery: Look at the audience for the opening question. Pause briefly after “English has.”*

## Slide 2 — One long history

**Target:** 1:10–2:10  
**Slide cue:** Old English → Middle English → Modern English → “Dates are boundaries, not switches.”

To see that long process, let's compare three snapshots of English. The first is Old English, from *Beowulf*. To most of us today, it looks almost like a foreign language.

The next is Middle English, from Chaucer. We recognise more words, but the grammar and pronunciation still feel quite different.

Finally, we have Shakespeare's English, an early form of Modern English. It is much easier to read, although a word like *wherefore* can still surprise us.

Quick question: which one is easiest for us today? Most of us would choose Shakespeare.

But people did not wake up one morning and replace their language. This is one long history. The dates on the slide are boundaries for study, not sudden switches.

*Delivery: Give the audience two or three seconds to look at the three passages before answering your own question.*

## Slide 3 — One sound changes many words

**Target:** 2:10–3:00  
**Slide cue:** `high · pie` → same vowel difference → many words

Within that continuous history, let's look at how sound change works. Does every word follow its own path?

Look at *high* and *pie*. In many Southern varieties of American English, the vowel sounds more like a long “ah.” In other varieties, we hear the vowel in *high* and *pie*.

You do not need to imitate either accent. Just notice that the difference appears in many words.

This is not one person's habit, and it is not one isolated word. It looks like one sound pattern acting across a group of words.

Once you notice that, the question changes. It is not “Why does this one word sound different?” It is “Why do so many words change in the same direction?”

*Delivery: Say “high, pie” once in your normal pronunciation. Do not attempt a Southern accent.*

## Slide 4 — The same change across history

**Target:** 3:00–3:50  
**Slide cue:** `house · out · south` → one historical sound change

History gives us exactly that kind of example. In Middle English, the word *house* had a long “oo” sound. It sounded more like “hoos.”

Later, that vowel changed to the sound we hear in *house* today. We see the same change in *out* and *south*.

These words do not have separate stories. They share one sound change.

So when linguists say a change is regular, they do not mean every word everywhere changes at once. They mean the same sound tends to change in a similar way across many words.

*Delivery: Read “hoos” then “house” slowly enough for the contrast to be audible.*

## Slide 5 — A pattern becomes evidence

**Target:** 3:50–4:55  
**Slide cue:** English `/f/` ↔ Spanish `/p/` → repeated matches → Part 3 handoff

That kind of regularity also helps us compare languages. In English, *foot* begins with /f/. In Spanish, *pie* (“PYEH,” meaning “foot”) begins with /p/.

English *fish* begins with /f/. Spanish *pez* (“PEHS,” meaning “fish”) begins with /p/.

You might say, “Two similar words? That could be chance.” Fair.

But when the same correspondence appears again and again, chance becomes much less likely. A repeated pattern becomes evidence.

The question is not whether two words look alike. It is whether their differences repeat in a predictable way.

Why this matters, and how linguists use it to reconstruct older languages, is the question our third speaker will take up.

*Delivery: Point separately to the English /f/ column and the Spanish /p/ column before explaining that the pattern, not visual similarity alone, is the evidence.*

## Slide 6 — The Great Vowel Shift

**Target:** 4:55–6:05  
**Slide cue:** long vowels move higher → *mice* / *mouse* → long vowels become diphthongs

Now, Before we move on to reconstruction, there is a more familiar example inside English itself: the Great Vowel Shift. Between about 1400 and 1600, many English long vowels changed their position in the mouth.

Many moved higher. But, The highest vowels could not move any higher, so they became diphthongs. A diphthong simply moves from one vowel position to another inside one syllable.

*Mice* used to have a long “ee.” Today we say /maɪs/.

*Mouse* used to have a long “oo.” Today we say /maʊs/.

So the sounds changed. But what happened to the spelling?

Not very much.

*Delivery: Slow down on the two old-to-new pairs. Pause after “But what happened to the spelling?” Then answer with a slight smile.*

## Slide 7 — Spelling as a museum

**Target:** 6:05–7:05  
**Slide cue:** spelling lags behind speech → *night* → `/x/` disappears

Writing changes much more slowly than speech, which is why English spelling can feel like a museum.

*Mice*, *house*, and *night* still contain traces of older English.

When you look at English spelling, you are often seeing an older stage of pronunciation through a glass window.

*Night* is a clear example. In Old English, the word was written *niht*, and the letters `gh` stood for a rough /x/ sound.

You do not need to reproduce it today. Imagine a breathy “h” made at the back of the mouth, a little like the `ch` in Scottish *loch*. Most modern English varieties lost that sound, but the spelling kept `gh`.

So spelling records history. It is not a recording of modern pronunciation.

*Delivery: You may audibly contrast /x/ with modern /naɪt/ once. Do not try to reconstruct the entire pre-modern word.*

## Slide 8 — A puzzle inside two words

**Target:** 7:05–7:55  
**Slide cue:** `divine` → /aɪ/, `divinity` → /ɪ/, same root → why?

That history inside spelling gives us another puzzle: look at *divine* and *divinity*.

The words are clearly related, but one has /aɪ/ and the other has /ɪ/.

Why would related words sound so different?

The answer is not that one word is irregular. The answer is time. English did not apply all its changes at once. Some changes happened earlier, and others came later.

At first this looks like an exception. But exceptions often become less mysterious once we recover their history.

*Delivery: Read “di-VINE” and “di-VIN-i-ty” slowly. Pause after the question.*

---

## Slide 9 — Changes happen in an order

**Target:** 7:55–8:55  
**Slide cue:** shortening happens first → Great Vowel Shift happens later → different outcomes

The order of the changes gives us the answer. First, an earlier rule shortened the vowel in *divinity*.

Later came the Great Vowel Shift. That shift affected long vowels.

*Divine* still had a long vowel, so it changed to /aɪ/. *Divinity* had already been shortened, so it had nothing long left for the later shift to affect.

We find the same history in *serene* and *serenity*, or *sane* and *sanity*.

This is why historical linguistics feels like detective work. We have evidence from today, and we try to recover the order of events that produced it.

*Delivery: Use the two visible routes on the slide instead of rereading the whole diagram.*

## Slide 10 — Endings carry grammatical jobs

**Target:** 8:55–10:05  
**Slide cue:** Latin *lupus* forms → Old English *stān* forms → ending marks the job

That same kind of historical reasoning also applies when we turn from sounds to grammar.

Many languages use endings on nouns to show a word's job in the sentence: who is acting, what is affected, or who receives something.

Latin does this. Its word for “wolf” changes form: *lupus*, *lupī*, *lupō*, *lupum*.

Old English did the same thing. Its word for “stone” could appear as *stān*, *stānes*, or *stāne*.

You do not need to memorise these forms. Just notice that the noun changes shape because its grammatical job changes. The ending is doing some of the grammar.

*Delivery: Circle two endings on the slide; do not read or translate every form.*

## Slide 11 — The old system wears out

**Target:** 10:05–10:50  
**Slide cue:** strong endings → weaker endings → meanings still need to be expressed

But over time, many of those endings became weaker, and then disappeared from ordinary nouns.

But the grammatical jobs did not disappear. Speakers still needed to say who did something, and who received something.

For a while, the old endings and the new strategies probably existed side by side.

So English had a problem. The old tools were wearing out, but the work still had to be done.

What did English do next?

*Delivery: Pause after the final question and move directly to the sentence pair on the next slide.*

## Slide 12 — Grammar redistributes the work

**Target:** 10:50–12:10  
**Slide cue:** same words, opposite event → word order → prepositions → grammar changes its tools

The answer was to rely more on word order. Look at these two sentences:

“The man saw the king.”

“The king saw the man.”

Almost the same words, but the event is reversed. What tells us the difference? Word order.

English also uses small words such as *to*, *of*, and *for*. In earlier English, noun endings carried more of this information. Modern English gives more weight to position and prepositions.

That is why English meaning is so sensitive to where a word appears in the sentence.

So English did not simply become easier. It redistributed the work. Some parts became simpler, while word order became more important and more restricted.

*Delivery: Point to the first noun in each sentence and say who is the actor before moving to the bottom summary.*

## Slide 13 — Three ideas and the handoff

**Target:** 12:10–13:10  
**Slide cue:** patterned → ordered → redistributed → Part 2

These examples all point to three ideas to take away.

First, sound change is patterned. It affects groups of words, not isolated accidents.

Second, changes interact, so their order matters. An earlier change can shape what a later change is able to do.

Third, when endings disappear, grammar moves its work to other tools, especially word order and prepositions.

In Part 1, we focused on sounds and word forms. But when endings disappear, what happens to the whole sentence? Our next speaker will show us how English changed its sentence structure, its vocabulary, and its meanings.

Thank you.

*Delivery: Pause after each of the three headings. Do not rush directly into the handoff.*
