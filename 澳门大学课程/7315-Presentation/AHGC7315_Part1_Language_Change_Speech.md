# AHGC7315 Part 1 — Language Change

**Presenter:** Part 1  
**Target time:** 13:00–13:30  
**Spoken length:** approximately 1,200 words  
**Style:** friendly, lightly humorous, clear, and conversational

> **中文辅助：** `〔中文：…〕` 只用于理解，不朗读。只在关键术语、文化背景和容易误解的地方标注，避免把整份英文稿变成逐句翻译。

## 1. The Hook

**Time:** 00:00–01:30

> **讲法：** 开场先看观众，不要立刻看稿。说到 “Where are you?” 时可以笑一下；说到 “The line has not changed” 时放慢。

Good morning, everyone. |

Before we begin, I have a small question. Have you ever seen Juliet's line, “Wherefore art thou Romeo?”〔中文：为什么你偏偏是罗密欧？其中 *wherefore* = 为什么〕 ||

Many people think Juliet is asking, “Romeo, where are you?” I understand. Imagine Juliet looking around and thinking, “Romeo, you are literally right there.” ||

But *wherefore* does not mean “where.” It means “why.” Juliet is asking why Romeo has to be a Montague.〔中文：Montague = 蒙太古家族〕 ||

The line has not changed. **English has.** ||

Today, we will look at how English changed, starting with its sounds. By the end of my part, I hope you will remember three ideas.

First, sound changes are usually **regular**, not random. Second, one change can affect **thousands of words** at the same time. And third, when English lost many word endings, its grammar had to move the work somewhere else.〔中文：把原本由词尾承担的语法功能转移到别处〕 ||

## 2. English Through Time

**Time:** 01:30–03:00

> **讲法：** 依次指向三栏，不要逐字朗读古英语。提问后停 2–3 秒，不需要听众举手；即使现场安静，也由你直接说出答案并继续。

Look at these three texts. The first is **Old English**〔中文：古英语〕, from *Beowulf*〔中文：《贝奥武夫》〕. To most of us today, it looks almost like a foreign language.

The next is **Middle English**〔中文：中古英语〕, from Chaucer〔中文：乔叟〕. We can recognize more words, but the grammar and pronunciation still feel quite different.

Finally, we have **Shakespeare's English**, an early form of Modern English〔中文：早期现代英语〕. It is much easier to read, although some words can still surprise us, as we just saw with *wherefore*. ||

Now, a quick question. Which one do you think would be easiest for us to understand today? ||

[Pause for 2–3 seconds.] Most of us would probably choose Shakespeare. | That makes sense. ||

The important point is that this is one long history. People did not wake up one morning and decide to replace their language. Changes moved slowly and spread across generations and communities. The dates on the slide are convenient boundaries, not sudden switches. ||

## 3. Sound Changes Are Patterned

**Time:** 03:00–05:20

> **讲法：** 这页不要陷入 IPA。先读 *high、pie、house、out、south*，再指对应符号。讲到共同祖先只留一句，完整方法交给 P3。

Here is our first big question. When the sounds of a language change, does every word follow its own path? ||

Usually, no. A sound change often affects the **same sound** across many words. That is why we can find a **regular sound correspondence**〔中文：规则语音对应〕.

Here is one example from American English. In many Southern varieties〔中文：美国南部方言〕, the vowel in **high** and **pie** sounds more like a long “ah.” In other dialects, it is the vowel in **high** and **pie**.

We do not need to imitate either accent. The important point is that the same difference appears across **many words**. It is not limited to one word or one person. ||

History gives us another example. In Middle English, the word **house** had a long “oo” sound. It sounded more like “hoos.” Later, that vowel became the sound we hear today in **house**. We can see the same pattern in **out** and **south**.

Because the pattern appears again and again, linguists describe it as **one sound change**, not dozens of unrelated changes. ||

Regular correspondences can also help us compare related languages. For example, English **father** begins with /f/, while Spanish **padre**〔中文：父亲〕 begins with /p/. English **fish** begins with /f/, and Spanish **pescado**〔中文：鱼〕 begins with /p/.

One match could be chance. Many matches together are much stronger evidence. Our third speaker will later explain how linguists use this evidence to reconstruct older languages〔中文：重建更古老的语言〕. ||

## 4. The Great Vowel Shift

**Time:** 05:20–07:50

> **讲法：** 先点标题，再指元音图。读 *mice* 和 *mouse* 时各读两遍，让大家听到旧长元音和现代双元音的区别。不要背整张表。

One of the most famous examples of sound change is the **Great Vowel Shift**〔中文：元音大推移〕. It happened in English between about fourteen hundred and sixteen hundred. During this period, many long vowels changed their position in the mouth.

Some vowels moved higher. The highest vowels could not move any higher, so they became **two-part vowel sounds**, or diphthongs〔中文：双元音〕. Do not worry if that word sounds difficult. It simply means that the vowel moves from one position to another inside one syllable.

For example, the vowel in **mice** used to sound like a long “ee.” Today, we say /maɪs/. The vowel in **mouse** used to sound like a long “oo.” Today, we say /maʊs/. ||

So the sounds changed. | But what happened to the spelling? ||

Well… | not very much. ||

Writing changed much more slowly than speech. That is why modern English spelling sometimes feels like a **museum**.〔中文：英语拼写像博物馆，保存了过去的语言形式〕 It still displays older stages of English, even when our pronunciation has moved on. ||

Sound changes can also make sounds disappear. Older English had a rough sound in words such as **night**.〔中文：这里指 /x/ 音〕 It was similar to the sound at the end of Scottish **loch**. Most modern English varieties no longer use that sound. Again, the pronunciation changed while the spelling stayed behind. ||

## 5. Traces Inside Words

**Time:** 07:50–10:00

> **讲法：** 读 *divine* 时重音在后：di-VINE；读 *divinity* 时重音在第二音节：di-VIN-i-ty。两句对比要清楚。

The past can also explain something strange inside related words. Look at **divine**〔中文：神圣的〕 and **divinity**〔中文：神性；神学〕. The first word has /aɪ/. The second word has /ɪ/.

These words are clearly related, so why do their vowels sound different? ||

The answer depends on **the order of changes**〔中文：变化发生的先后顺序〕. First, an earlier rule shortened the vowel in **divinity**. Later, the Great Vowel Shift affected **long vowels**. The vowel in **divine** was still long, so it changed. The vowel in **divinity** had already been shortened, so it did not change in the same way.

We can find the same history in pairs such as **serene**〔中文：宁静的〕 and **serenity**〔中文：宁静〕, or **sane**〔中文：神志清醒的〕 and **sanity**〔中文：理智；神志清醒〕.

This is the main lesson. A sound change is not just a list of old sounds and new sounds. Changes happen in a sequence. One change can create the conditions for the next change, or protect a word from a later change.

That is why historical linguistics is a little like detective work. We look at the evidence that is still here and try to work out the order of events that produced it. ||

## 6. From Sound to Grammar

**Time:** 10:00–13:10

> **讲法：** 不需要朗读 slide 上全部拉丁语或古英语形式。只指 slide，说 “same noun, different endings”。最后两段放慢，准备交接。

Finally, let's move from sounds to **grammar**.

Many languages use endings on nouns to show a word's grammatical job〔中文：语法作用〕. The ending may tell us who is doing an action, what is being acted on, or who receives something. Latin did this, and Old English did it too. ||

On the slide, you can see one Latin noun for “wolf.” It has different endings in different contexts. You do not need to memorize the forms. Just notice the main idea: the noun changes shape because its grammatical role changes.

Old English also had these endings. Its word for **stone** appears with different endings when it is used in different grammatical contexts. ||

Over time, many of those endings became weaker and eventually disappeared from ordinary nouns. But speakers still needed to express the same meanings. So what happened? ||

English began to rely more heavily on **word order**〔中文：语序〕 and **prepositions**〔中文：介词〕. Compare these two sentences: “The man saw the king,” and “The king saw the man.” The words are almost the same, but the meaning changes completely, because the order tells us who did what.

Earlier English could rely more on noun endings. Modern English relies more on position and little words such as **to**, **of**, and **for**. ||

Does that mean English simply became easier? Not really. Some parts became simpler, but word order became more important and more restricted. English did not stop doing grammatical work. It started carrying that work with different tools. ||

So here are the three ideas I hope you will remember. First, sound changes are usually **patterned**. Second, different changes can interact, so the order of events matters. And third, a change in one part of the language can force another part to take over its work.

In Part 1, we looked at sounds and word forms. But when endings disappear, what happens to the whole sentence? ||

Our next speaker will show us how English changed its sentence structure, its vocabulary, and its meanings. ||

Thank you.
